# Probability Density Functions

The exercises in this chapter practice the central idea of continuous probability: that probability for a continuous random variable is not concentrated at points but spread as *density* across intervals. The two complementary tools explored here — computing area under a PDF and evaluating a CDF — are two views of the same underlying integral, and the exercises systematically build the intuition that $P(a < X < b) = \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)$. The chapter also introduces kernel density estimation as a way to let data speak for itself without committing to a parametric family.

## Exercise: Exponential Waiting Times for Soccer Goals

The exponential distribution with rate $\lambda$ has PDF $f(t) = \lambda e^{-\lambda t}$ and CDF $F(t) = 1 - e^{-\lambda t}$ for $t \geq 0$. It is the canonical model for waiting times between events in a Poisson process — that is, when events arrive independently at a constant average rate $\lambda$. Here, $\lambda = 2.5$ goals per game means an expected wait of $1/\lambda = 0.4$ games before the first goal.

The solution computes $P(T < 0.5)$ two ways. `area_under(pdf_model, 0, 0.5)` performs numerical integration of the PDF over $[0, 0.5]$: it evaluates the density at a grid of points and applies a quadrature rule (effectively summing $f(t)\,\Delta t$ slices). `ExponentialCdf(lam=2.5)(0.5)` instead evaluates the closed-form antiderivative $F(0.5) = 1 - e^{-2.5 \times 0.5} \approx 0.713$ directly. The two results agree to many decimal places, with the tiny residual difference attributable to floating-point rounding in numerical integration rather than any conceptual mismatch.

For the second-half probability, $P(0.5 < T < 1)$, the PDF route integrates over $[0.5, 1]$ and the CDF route computes $F(1) - F(0.5)$. This CDF subtraction is not a trick — it is the exact mathematical content of "area under the PDF between two points." Notice that this second probability (~0.204) is much smaller than the first-half probability (~0.713). This reflects the exponential's rapid decay: most probability mass is concentrated near zero, consistent with the memoryless property — conditioned on no goal in the first half, the probability of a goal in the next interval depends only on that interval's length, not on the scoreless first half. A hidden assumption throughout: the Poisson model requires that goals arrive independently and at a truly constant rate, which in real soccer is violated by factors like game state, fatigue, and tactical changes.

## Exercise: Normal Heights and KDE for Blue Man Group Eligibility

Before fitting any model, the solution calls `scipy.stats.trimboth(heights, 0.01)`, which symmetrically removes the bottom and top 1% of the sorted data. This is a form of *robust preprocessing*: survey height data frequently contain entry errors at the extremes (impossible values, unit confusion), and even a handful of extreme outliers can substantially inflate the sample standard deviation, distorting the fitted normal distribution. The sample mean and standard deviation computed from the trimmed array are therefore more representative of the bulk of the distribution. Note that `np.std` with no `ddof` argument returns the *population* standard deviation (dividing by $n$, not $n-1$), which is the maximum-likelihood estimator for $\sigma$ under a normal model.

The `NormalCdf(m, s)` object evaluates $F(x) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)$, where $\Phi$ is the standard normal CDF. Plotting it against the empirical step-function CDF of the raw data is a direct goodness-of-fit check: if the normal model fits well, the two curves will be nearly indistinguishable over the central bulk, with possible divergence only in the extreme tails.

`gaussian_kde` from `scipy.stats` estimates a nonparametric PDF by placing a Gaussian kernel of width $h$ at each data point: $\hat{f}(x) = \frac{1}{nh} \sum_{i=1}^n \phi\!\left(\frac{x - x_i}{h}\right)$, where $\phi$ is the standard normal PDF. The `bw_method=0.25` argument scales the default Scott's-rule bandwidth by a factor of 0.25, producing a narrower kernel and therefore a less-smooth (more data-following) estimate. The critical assumption here is that the true density is smooth enough for a Gaussian kernel to approximate it well. Because the BRFSS height data is recorded in whole centimeters, there is a discretization artifact that KDE smooths away — an appropriate smoothing level hides this rounding while not erasing genuine shape.

Computing the Blue Man Group eligible fraction via both `area_under(pdf_normal_model, 178, 185)` and `cdf_normal_model(185) - cdf_normal_model(178)` yields ~0.343, while the empirical CDF gives ~0.335. The close agreement between parametric model and raw data validates the normal assumption for the bulk of male heights. The small discrepancy reflects that the normal model is an idealization: real height distributions have slightly heavier tails and some skew. A key unstated assumption is that the BRFSS sample is representative of the U.S. male adult population — if the survey has systematic coverage gaps (e.g., underrepresentation of very tall or very short subgroups), the fraction estimate will be biased.
